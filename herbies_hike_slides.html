Before the simulator · the story

A plant in trouble and a scout hike

In Goldratt and Cox's The Goal, plant manager Alex Rogo has three months to turn his factory around or it closes.

One weekend he leads his son's scout troop on a hike. The boys start in a neat line, but it keeps stretching out. The leaders get far ahead, the troop falls behind schedule, and Alex realises the line behaves like his plant.

Near the middle walks Herbie: a willing boy with the heaviest pack, and the slowest walker in the troop.

Before the simulator · how Goldratt measures a system

Three measures, on the trail and in the plant

Throughput

Money coming in

The rate at which the system makes money through sales.

On the trail: the pace of the last hiker. The troop has only arrived when the last one has.

Inventory

Money tied up

Money invested in things the system intends to sell.

On the trail: the length of the line, front to back.

Operating expense

Money going out

Money spent turning inventory into throughput.

On the trail: all eight hikers, on the clock whoever is fastest.

Goal: raise throughput while cutting inventory and operating expense. The simulator shows all three.

These are the measures Jonah gives Alex in the book. The simulator's three cards use exactly these names.

The simulator · three ways to hike

Own pace, the drum, buffer and rope

Own pace

Everyone walks their own speed

Herbie sits in the middle of the line. This is the troop before anyone fixes anything.

The drum

Herbie walks in front

His footsteps set the beat. Everyone faster stacks up behind him instead of running ahead.

Buffer & rope

A rope ties them in order

The line can never be longer than the rope. The slack just behind Herbie is the buffer.

In the book, Alex moves Herbie to the front, then shares out the heavy load in his pack. The rope is how Goldratt later turned the lesson into a scheduling method: drum-buffer-rope.

DBR as a named method comes from Goldratt and Fox, The Race (1986), building on The Goal.

Only work on the constraint moves the whole troop.

Time saved at a non-constraint changes nothing. Time lost or gained at the constraint is lost or gained by the whole system.

From the trail to the plant

Drum-buffer-rope scheduling

On the trailIn the plant
DrumHerbie's footsteps set the paceThe constraint's schedule sets the pace for the whole plant
BufferSlack in the rope just behind HerbieA time buffer of work waiting in front of the constraint, so it never starves
RopeTies every hiker to the one aheadMaterial is released only as fast as the constraint consumes it

The rope is a pull signal: release follows the constraint, not a forecast. It is the same idea as the WIP cap in the push vs pull session.

Buffers in DBR are usually sized in time (hours of work in front of the constraint), not in units.
The five focusing steps · on the trail

Elevate, then look for the next Herbie

  1. Identify the constraint: Herbie, the slowest hiker.
  2. Exploit it: put him in front and never let anything hold him up.
  3. Subordinate everyone else to his pace: the rope and the buffer.
  4. Elevate it: share out his pack. The troop now arrives in 2:29 instead of 3:25.
  5. Repeat: with the pack shared, the rope holds Herbie back for about 30 s per hike. The constraint has moved to other hikers.
Try it · Herbie's Hike simulatorOn Buffer & rope, press Share Herbie's pack and watch for "The rope is taut. Herbie is stopped."mean of 200 hikes
Step 5 in the book also warns against inertia: don't let yesterday's fix become today's constraint.
Takeaways

Three things to remember

  • 1The system's output is set by its constraint. Speeding up anyone else only adds inventory and waiting.
  • 2Drum-buffer-rope keeps output at the constraint's pace with far less inventory: pace with the drum, protect it with a buffer, release with the rope.
  • 3Improve the constraint, then look again: the next Herbie is already in the line.
