90-Day
Factory Flow
Improvement Plan

A focused, shop-floor-led programme to improve the movement of work through a manufacturing line: identify the constraint, protect and improve its productive time, align upstream and downstream activity, and establish a repeatable daily management system.

90 daysDiscover • Improve • Sustain
One pilot lineStart with a defined product family
Cross-functionalOperations, engineering, quality, planning
Programme intent
Deliver measurable improvement in throughput, lead time, work-in-progress (WIP), schedule adherence and quality—without assuming that buying more equipment or keeping every machine busy is the answer.

What success looks like

↑

More good output

Increase good units completed and shipped through the whole value stream, not simply activity at individual machines.

↓

Less waiting and WIP

Reduce queues, excess batch sizes, avoidable movement and time spent waiting between operations.

↗

More reliable delivery

Improve plan attainment, visibility of risk and the ability to recover quickly from disruption.

✓

Capability to sustain

Equip supervisors and operators with standards, visual controls and a regular problem-solving rhythm.

Improvement principles

1. Optimise the whole flow

Prioritise end-to-end customer flow over isolated machine utilisation or departmental efficiency.

2. Follow the evidence

Use actual shop-floor observation and consistent data. Validate the apparent bottleneck before acting.

3. Involve the people doing the work

Operators, team leaders and support functions co-design countermeasures and test them in short cycles.

4. Improve safely and sustainably

Quality, safety, maintenance and process control are conditions of improvement—not trade-offs.

The 90-day deployment

From diagnosis to controlled flow

The sequence is deliberately staged. Days are indicative working days from mobilisation; adjust to shift patterns, product mix and data availability.

DAYS
1–15

Phase 1 — Understand the current state

Discover & baseline

  • Agree the business problem, customer requirements, pilot product family, line boundaries and sponsor.
  • Go to the shop floor across relevant shifts; observe actual work, queues, hand-offs, changeovers and stoppages.
  • Build a current-state value stream map covering material and information flow; record cycle times, changeover, uptime, yield, WIP and end-to-end lead time.
  • Establish a baseline and validate demand, available production time, takt time and the line's demonstrated capacity.
  • Identify the likely system constraint using queue patterns, capacity and lost-time evidence—not utilisation alone.

Deliverables: agreed scope and charter; current-state map; baseline KPI pack; prioritised loss/constraint analysis.

DAYS
16–30

Phase 2 — Design the first improvements

Prioritise & plan

  • Confirm the constraint and quantify its main losses: breakdowns, setups, minor stops, speed loss, defects, material or staffing gaps.
  • Apply the Theory of Constraints five focusing steps: identify, exploit, subordinate, elevate where justified, then repeat.
  • Protect constraint time: planned maintenance, correct tooling, material availability, quality checks before the constraint and rapid escalation of stoppages.
  • Design a practical future-state flow: sensible WIP limits, clear FIFO lanes, replenishment signals and a controlled production release point.
  • Select 3–5 improvement experiments with named owners, expected effect, risks and verification method.

Deliverables: constraint action plan; future-state map; 30/60/90-day action tracker; agreed KPI definitions and targets.

DAYS
31–60

Phase 3 — Implement and learn

Execute rapid improvement cycles

  • Run short Plan–Do–Check–Act (PDCA) trials on the largest constraint losses; review results daily and adapt.
  • Use SMED principles to separate internal/external setup tasks, prepare tools/materials in advance and standardise changeovers.
  • Introduce basic equipment care and focused maintenance actions on the constraint; record downtime consistently.
  • Balance work to demand where appropriate; remove avoidable walking, searching, handling and rework.
  • Pilot visual production control: hourly plan versus actual, constraint status, WIP limits, quality alerts and escalation triggers.
  • Train teams in revised standard work; verify that changes work across shifts and product variants.

Deliverables: implemented countermeasures; updated standards; visual management board; evidence of impact and outstanding barriers.

DAYS
61–90

Phase 4 — Stabilise, verify and hand over

Sustain & scale

  • Confirm gains against the baseline using comparable demand, mix, shift and planned-time assumptions.
  • Reassess the constraint: if it has moved, repeat the TOC cycle rather than continuing to optimise yesterday's bottleneck.
  • Refine scheduling, buffer sizing and material replenishment based on observed flow and delivery risk.
  • Embed tiered daily/weekly reviews, named KPI owners, abnormality response and an active improvement backlog.
  • Complete a lessons-learned review and identify the next line, product family or upstream/downstream process for deployment.

Deliverables: 90-day results report; control plan; trained process owners; next-quarter improvement roadmap and scale-up recommendation.

Measurement, governance & practical tools

How progress will be measured

Agree definitions before baseline collection. Use a small, balanced set of measures that shows whether the whole line is improving. Targets should be set after the first 10–15 days of observation; the figures below are not guaranteed outcomes.

MeasureDefinition / useReview
Good output / throughputGood units completed (and shipped where relevant) per shift/day; track mix and demand alongside it.Each shift / daily
End-to-end lead timeElapsed time from release/start to completion or dispatch for the selected product family.Weekly
WIPUnits or value between defined process points; show queue size at the constraint.Each shift / daily
Constraint performanceAvailable time, downtime reason, productive run time, output and good-part yield at the constraint.Each shift
OEE (where useful)Availability × Performance × Quality; use to understand losses, not as a standalone business goal.Daily / weekly
Right-first-time / scrapGood units as a proportion of total; record defects and rework by cause and process step.Daily / weekly
Schedule attainment / OTIFActual completion against the agreed production plan and customer delivery commitment.Daily / weekly
Changeover timeRepeatable elapsed time from last good part to first good part of next run.Per changeover

Core team and responsibilities

  • Sponsor: sets direction, removes barriers and approves resources.
  • Improvement lead: coordinates the plan, data, workshops and action log.
  • Production / team leaders: lead daily control and standard work.
  • Operators: identify problems, test and sustain practical changes.
  • Engineering / maintenance: reliability, tooling and changeover improvements.
  • Quality / planning / materials: defect prevention, schedule and supply support.

Methods used selectively

  • Theory of Constraints: focus on the system constraint.
  • Value Stream Mapping: see end-to-end material and information flow.
  • Drum–Buffer–Rope / buffer management: protect the constraint and control release where appropriate.
  • Lean / PDCA: remove waste and test countermeasures.
  • SMED: reduce changeover losses.
  • TPM, standard work and visual management: stabilise and sustain.
  • Digital dashboards / simple data capture: improve visibility where data quality and use case justify it.
Important implementation note
This is a generic manufacturing-line framework, not a diagnosis of a particular factory. Final scope, targets, staffing and interventions should be confirmed after site observation and baseline measurement. Do not set an arbitrary OEE target or invest in extra capacity until the constraint and its losses are evidenced. Safety, regulatory requirements and product quality remain non-negotiable.
